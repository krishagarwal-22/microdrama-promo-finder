Why this problem, and who it serves

An editor hand-picks promo moments from every episode of every show, one at a time, and new shows ship weekly — the work grows linearly with the catalogue and is the clearest bottleneck of the three briefs. It also has the richest signal: timestamped screenplays I can read deterministically. Crucially, the audience is well-defined — US microdramas are driven by women aged ~30–60, documented as especially drawn to romance and revenge narratives (The Micro-Drama Economy report; TheWrap/Similarweb) — and this show is exactly that: a betrayal-at-the-altar revenge drama. That told me not just where to cut, but which moments this specific audience rewards. The tool proposes a ranked shortlist of promo clips with exact in/out timestamps, turning the editor's job from "watch everything and find" into "review a few and approve."

What it does

Point it at the scripts; it returns, per promo: episode, start/end timestamp of each segment, the hook (first 3s), the cliffhanger it cuts on, one line on why, and a 0–100 score — ranked across the show. On the pack it found 97 promos across all 50 episodes in under a second; 28 carry a full wrong→payback arc.

How it works

Free, deterministic rules do the finding; judgment is isolated so a model can be swapped in:

  • Parse — scripts are regular (type, speaker, emotion tag, in/out time per beat), so no AI is needed to read them.
  • Score each beat — a cheap hook score (hot emotion, shock words, short punchy lines) and cliffhanger score (unanswered question, "cut to black", score swell, episode end).
  • Generate candidates — for each strong hook beat, take the best cliffhanger 20–60s later; the window between is a candidate.
  • Judge — craft rubric (hook 35 · cliffhanger 30 · stands-alone 20 · intensity 15), then an audience-resonance layer that lifts injustice→vindication clips (below). --scorer llm swaps the judge for an API model.
  • Dedupe & rank — drop overlaps, keep best ~2/episode, sort all.

Why these numbers (grounded, not guessed)

  • 3-second hook: platforms treat the first 3s as a distinct ranking signal; ~40% of viewers swipe within it and >70% decide whether to stay (TikTok retention data).
  • 20–60s length: the platform sweet spot; ~72% of TikToks run under 60s and reach falls off past 90s.
  • Cliffhanger: Loewenstein's information-gap theory (a knowledge gap is a cognitive "itch") and the Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks hold tension) — honest caveat: Zeigarnik's recall claim replicates weakly, so I lean on the tension, not memory.
  • Which emotions: Berger & Milkman (2012, JMR) — high-arousal emotions travel, low-arousal sadness doesn't.
  • Audience-resonance: Affective Disposition Theory (Weber et al. 2008, tested on 500+ female serial-drama viewers) — enjoyment peaks on poetic justice: a wronged, liked character vindicated / the villain punished.

Approach & scale

The expensive judgment (watching) is replaced by cheap rules that do the finding; a model is optional and only re-ranks a short list. The core has zero dependencies and runs instantly, so cost is dominated by the optional LLM call. At real volume — say 20 shows/week × 50 episodes, ~2 candidates each — that's ~2,000 short re-rank calls a week on a mini model: cents, not an editor-week. The model is a one-flag swap (PROMO_LLM_MODEL env var) and falls back to the free scorer if no key is set, so a batch never breaks and a stronger model you provide drops straight in. The human stays in the loop: the tool proposes ranked clips with exact timestamps; the editor approves or nudges the in/out points.

How I know it's good — and where it fails

I ran it on the full pack and read the top picks against the scripts. #1 (Ep 38) opens on "Do you even know what happened to me?" and carries a full wrong→payback arc; #2 (Ep 18, 02:38–03:12) opens on "Someone wanted us out." after the arson and cuts to black on a vow of vengeance. Adding the poetic-justice layer re-ranked 11 of the top 15 toward betrayal-and-vindication clips — the arcs this audience is documented to prefer — while keeping the verified-strong clips. Sanity: durations 20–59s (median 38), every episode covered, 36/97 cut on an unanswered question, 28/97 carry a full wrong+payback arc.

Where it picks badlyWhy / fix
The resonance signal is keyword-based: it can miss poetic justice that is shown, not said. My first (aggressive) weight even buried the excellent Ep 18 clip — I dialled it back until it returned to #2.This is the clearest case for the swappable LLM scorer, which reads meaning, not keywords. The weight itself is a hand-tuned judgment call.
"Stands-alone" is coarse (only returns 0.70 or 0.85).Rules can't tell if a stranger follows the plot; an LLM judges this far better.
Purely visual hooks (a silent slap) score low; intensity is a weak differentiator.Kept at low weight and flagged; audio/vision would fix it.

Tools used, and one place AI got it wrong

I used Claude to scaffold the parser/engine and pressure-test the scoring, and to gather the audience and psychology sources (which I then checked). One concrete miss: its first intensity metric scored almost every clip at ~0.10 — too harsh, and the scripts tag emotion mainly on dialogue — so intensity was a near-useless differentiator. Rather than tune it into a fake signal I dropped its weight and documented it. The scoring design, the 20–60s/continuous-clip decisions, grounding the weights in the audience + Affective Disposition Theory, and the hand-tuning of the resonance weight are mine.
